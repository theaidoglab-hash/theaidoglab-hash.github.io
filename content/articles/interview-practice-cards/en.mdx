## One question has one job

An interview answer becomes thin when it tries to show every tool at once. This quick start points to one practice question per card. Each question page starts with an interviewer-style situation and a plausible answer that is only partly right. It then asks you to name the missing mechanism, the trade-off, the condition in which the approach should stop, and an artefact that could survive follow-up questions.

Choose a card from the verbs in a role description, not from the title alone. `build`, `integrate`, and `ship` usually point to product and system decisions. `operate`, `secure`, and `scale` usually point to reliability, permissions, cost, and incidents. `experiment` and `measure` usually point to baselines, data boundaries, and evaluation. Choose the route that matches the most repeated verbs. You do not need to finish all 16 cards in one sitting.

## A 90-second rehearsal

Use a timer. The first pass must be your own language, not a memorised script.

1. **First 20 seconds — frame the decision.** Say who needs to decide what, what success would look like, and one non-goal. If the question begins with a technical symptom, turn it into an observable failure before naming a tool.
2. **Next 40 seconds — explain the path.** Give the simplest credible baseline, the mechanism or component you would inspect first, and the trade-off that would change your choice. The sequence matters: observe, make a small reversible change, then escalate only if evidence warrants it.
3. **Final 30 seconds — show that the work is safe to follow.** Name one test, trace, decision record, or demo artefact. End with a boundary: human review, rollback, a data limit, or the case in which the simpler option is better.

Only then read the individual page. Do not replace your answer wholesale; add the missing causal link. Keep a four-line note: **claim, evidence, boundary, next test**. Repeat the same question tomorrow. If the answer becomes shorter and more precise, you are learning the decision rather than reciting vocabulary.

## The evidence check before you say “I built this”

An interviewer can ask both how a system works and why it deserved to exist. Your evidence should answer both.

### Show how the system behaves

- A small baseline and the situation in which the more complex design beats it.
- A data or input boundary: what is allowed, excluded, synthetic, stale, or unknown.
- Fixed evaluation cases, expected behaviour, and at least one retained failure case.
- A trace, state diagram, or version record that shows how you would investigate a regression.
- Run instructions and a README that separate observed output from a performance claim.

### Also say what must be clear before it is used

- The user decision, the owner of the last consequential step, and a non-goal.
- A practical measure such as reviewer capacity, handling time, error exposure, or handoff rate—not invented revenue.
- The operational or cost trade-off you accepted and the simpler option you rejected.
- A stop rule: when to ask a person, roll back, limit a feature, or decline an unsupported action.
- An honest demo boundary and a note about what was not tested in a real organisation.

If no artefact exists yet, do not add more model names to the answer. Build the smallest missing item: one fixture, one evaluation row, one decision record, or one recovery test. That is usually more useful than another hour of reading.

## Scope of this practice series

The questions, fictional situations, explanations, and suggested artefacts here are draft learning material. They are not a record of any company’s current process, a company-by-company question bank, or a prediction of any interview loop. Practise with public, fictional, synthetic, or properly de-identified material only. Do not turn an invented scenario into a client story, use confidential work as proof, or claim a production deployment or business outcome that you cannot substantiate. The point is to make sound engineering judgement inspectable.
