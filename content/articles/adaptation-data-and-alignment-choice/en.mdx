“The model is wrong, so I would fine-tune it” is only half an answer. Before changing weights, decide whether the failure comes from missing context, an unclear task, unstable labels, or a behaviour that truly repeats across lawful examples.

## The fictional warranty desk

Consider a draft-only warranty desk. A reviewer pastes a de-identified claim summary. The system must return either a structured evidence request or **HANDOFF**. It must not approve a claim, look up a real account, or invent a policy.

Three failures arrive in the same week. One draft cites an out-of-date policy. One cannot distinguish a missing photo from a denied claim. One gives a long explanation in the wrong language. These look like “model quality” failures, but their remedies differ.

- A current policy belongs in versioned retrieval, not training data.
- A missing-photo decision may need a clearer output schema and fixed examples.
- A consistent writing style may be a prompting or controlled-template problem.
- A repeated, stable transformation with enough reviewed examples may justify adaptation.

## Adaptation is a data decision

**Supervised fine-tuning** teaches a model from input-output examples. **Parameter-efficient fine-tuning**, such as adapter-style methods, changes a smaller set of trainable parameters. **Preference optimisation** uses comparisons such as “draft A was preferred to draft B” to steer a defined behaviour. These names do not remove the need for a task definition, a lawful data route, and a held-out evaluation set.

“Alignment” should not be used as shorthand for “safe now.” At a practical level, it means specifying and measuring desired behaviour: when to produce a draft, when to decline, how to use a supplied language, and what evidence must be present. Permissions, data minimisation, external action controls, and human approval remain system responsibilities outside the model weights.

## Build the data gate before the training job

Create a small adaptation decision packet:

1. **Task card:** input, expected output, non-goals, risk owner, and a no-model baseline.
2. **Data card:** source, consent or licence basis, de-identification process, label guide, date range, and known gaps.
3. **Split record:** separate train, validation, and held-out test cases by source or time where leakage is plausible.
4. **Evaluation matrix:** ordinary cases, missing evidence, conflicting policy, multilingual inputs, refusal cases, and output-schema failures.
5. **Change record:** baseline result, candidate result, regression list, cost/latency trade-off, and a stop or rollback condition.

Do not let resolved reviewer outcomes silently become labels. They may contain information unavailable when a new claim arrives. Do not mix today’s policy into an old training split and call a better score generalisation. A held-out case is only useful if its answer was not already encoded by a duplicate, near-duplicate, or future-only field.

## Choose the smaller fix first

If a versioned policy passage fixes the stale-policy case, retrieval is the better change. If a strict JSON validator catches the missing-photo mistake, keep the model unchanged. If human reviewers cannot agree on the label, more training examples will amplify ambiguity rather than solve it.

Fine-tuning can be appropriate when the task is stable, labels are reviewed, enough cases cover real variation, and the before-and-after comparison includes correct handoffs and harmful regressions. Even then, retain the prompt, data version, model version, evaluation slice, and release decision. A model that learns a concise tone does not thereby earn authority to decide a warranty claim.

## Evidence that travels to an interview

Keep the data card, split logic, baseline, evaluation harness, and reproducible change report in the repository. Also record the purpose limitation, who owns the labels, the policy-freshness boundary, the reviewer route, and when weak evidence means not training. That is a stronger portfolio story than “I fine-tuned a model on a dataset.”

## Boundary

The warranty desk, labels, policies, and results in this lesson are fictional. This article does not prescribe training data, legal authority, provider settings, or a production release. A prototype evaluation is evidence about named cases, not proof of fairness, safety, adoption, or business impact.
