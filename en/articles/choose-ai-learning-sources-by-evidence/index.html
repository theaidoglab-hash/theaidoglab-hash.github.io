Start with a gap, then check that the course gives you a practical task, usable feedback, and a next step to record.

Before choosing an AI course, name one piece of work you will keep this week. If a source does not leave you something a target-role reader can rerun, understand, and ask about, a certificate or tool list is not enough.

Start with the gap

“I want to learn AI” is not a comparable need. Start with one gap in a target role or project. Perhaps you can write prompts but not design evaluation. Perhaps you understand a notebook but cannot explain data, versions and deployment. Perhaps you have a RAG demo but no business problem, permission boundary or failure cases.

A course list can help you discover directions, but it cannot prove that one course fits you or automatically become job evidence. AI.DOG treats learning sources as means: decide which artefact you want to retain, then choose the format worth investing in.

What different sources can actually give you

  • Official documentation, papers and open-source READMEs: accurate concepts, API boundaries and rerunnable minimum examples. They lack rhythm and feedback, so you define the project and acceptance yourself.
  • Structured courses with assignments: sequencing, practice pressure and sometimes a rubric or peer feedback. Their value depends on whether an assignment can become a showable artefact, not whether the title sounds fashionable.
  • Guided builds, workshops or cohorts: useful when you are stuck on problem decomposition, code review or explaining trade-offs. Check whether feedback applies to your own delivery rather than a generic livestream.
  • Public-data challenges, hackathons or volunteer builds: an opportunity to practise collaboration and incomplete requirements, provided you establish data rights, team contribution and publication rights first. Do not claim the whole team outcome as yours.
  • A self-directed portfolio sprint: the most direct way to close a target-role gap. It requires self-management, but each step can connect to a README, evaluation report and interview explanation.

None is inherently superior. If your gap is evaluation, a second prompt course may be less useful than a fixed test set and error analysis.

Run six checks before enrolling

1. Which exact gap does it close?

Write a falsifiable sentence, such as: “After this I can write a baseline, test set, metric definitions and release gate for a synthetic RAG case.” If all you can write is “I will understand GenAI better,” the scope is still too broad.

2. Which artefact will I retain?

Decide before the course begins: a problem brief, data card, evaluation report, architecture note, rerunnable notebook, decision log or a README that clearly separates your contribution. Keep the certificate if useful, but it cannot be the only artefact.

3. Is there feedback, and can I use it?

Inspect assignments, rubrics, office hours, code review, project critique and the boundary around sample solutions. Feedback that only reports completion may not improve your understanding of failure modes or trade-offs.

4. How does it verify understanding rather than clicking completion?

Prefer work that asks you to explain a decision, compare a baseline, handle a failure or let someone else rerun the work. Multiple choice and video copying can be a start, but they are weak as your only evidence.

5. Are time, money and update burden proportionate?

Count total cost: lessons, assignments, tools, cloud, travel, opportunity cost from delaying applications, and the likelihood that material becomes stale. Expensive does not guarantee feedback; free does not mean valueless.

6. Is there a stop condition?

Stop or switch to a smaller learning unit if, after four weeks, there is no first inspectable artefact; the course is unrelated to the target role; material cannot be lawfully published; or the assignment only reproduces an instructor answer. Stopping is not failure. It prevents sunk cost from replacing judgement.

Turn the course into an eight-week work plan

In week one, choose a role and one evidence gap. In week two, write a project brief and baseline. In weeks three and four, make the smallest rerunnable implementation using course material. In week five, add evaluation cases. In week six, record failures, cost and data boundaries. In week seven, ask a peer or reviewer to challenge it with a rubric. In week eight, assemble the README, decision log and a five-minute walkthrough.

This cadence does not guarantee a job. It does ensure that a worthwhile investment leaves an inspectable output rather than merely another completion record.

Example: make a bounded case from a RAG course

Suppose the course teaches putting documents into a vector index. Turn the assignment into a small PolicyPilot slice: write fictional policy data, make a keyword baseline, add region-specific metadata filters, build no-answer and injection tests, and explain why the system cannot issue refunds. The tools may be identical, but you add a business boundary, evaluation and human responsibility. That creates engineering judgement worth probing.

What a course can actually help with

A strong course reduces curriculum-design effort, supplies feedback and deadlines, and introduces concepts you may not have found alone. It cannot choose your problem, validate your result, manage privacy or describe your contribution for you. Treat it as a source of material and rhythm, not an identity label.
