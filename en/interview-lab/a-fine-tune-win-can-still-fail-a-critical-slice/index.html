A fine-tune win can still fail a critical slice

Answer once from your own experience. Then read the notes: they unpack how the system works, the trade-offs at work, and when it should stop.

CHECK YOUR ANSWER

What should this answer cover?

Do not memorise a script. After answering, use these six prompts to check that you named the scenario, tempting wrong answer, mechanism, trade-off, failure, and evidence another person can inspect.

The situation
A fine-tune raises the overall score but makes errors more frequent in a high-consequence request slice.
The tempting wrong answer
Letting the overall score hide a critical-slice regression and releasing anyway.
The mechanism to explain
Slices, hard gates, baselines, confidence, human review, rollback triggers, and release records.
The trade-off to name
A hard gate can cost overall score or rollout speed, in exchange for not handing a known risk to users.
One way it can fail
Average performance improves while a small critical case set gains unacceptable errors.
Evidence someone can inspect
A slice-level evaluation table with explicit reject-release, handoff, or rollback rules.

Interviewer asks: A fine-tune raises its overall score on a held-out set from 82 to 88, but errors in a high-consequence request slice rise from 1/40 to 5/40. The product owner says 88 passes the release target. What decision and evidence would you propose?

Half-right answer: The total score is above target, so the fine-tune is better. The critical slice is small; observe it again next round.

The total score may genuinely rise, but it does not automatically decide which errors can be handed to users. A slice is a group of cases defined by a meaningful work condition: strict formatting, a particular language, long context, sensitive action, or requests needing human review. A critical slice is not a deliberately hard group chosen to embarrass the model. It states in advance that errors there have higher consequences than a typo or ordinary retry. It needs its own baseline, enough cases, acceptable-error boundary, and owner.

A total score is a class average, not the most important exam

If a student’s language, general knowledge, and sport average rises while their only safety exam changes from pass to fail, a teacher does not report only an improved average. A cohort score can describe an overall trend, but it cannot erase a non-exchangeable requirement. AI evaluation is similar. An overall score can rank candidates, while a hard gate is a rule that good performance elsewhere cannot offset: for example, “the critical schema success rate may not fall below baseline” or “handoff coverage for a high-risk request cannot fall below an agreed floor.”

Write a hard gate before seeing candidate results, or enthusiasm for the new model will make it easy to relax later. Be honest about the sample too: 5/40 may not precisely estimate a true error rate, so report the count, slice definition, data boundary, and uncertainty. But “the sample is small” is not a reason to pretend a visible high-consequence regression does not exist. The next step may be to add representative cases, narrow the rollout, retain human review, or reject the candidate—not to relabel uncertainty as safe.

Ask why the slice exists

Every critical slice should describe a work path: who sees the output, which decision uses it, what a failure causes, and which simpler baseline remains safe. A formatting error may merely need a retry. If it makes a downstream review miss a required field, automatic completion should stop and the reviewer should be shown the issue. That makes the slice part of product and risk design, rather than a decoration on an evaluation dashboard.

Freeze the candidate, prompt or adapter config, data version, and evaluation harness. For each slice, record score, case count, error type, trace, human-review conclusion, and whether a fallback can repair it. If the overall score rises while a critical slice regresses, the default decision is do not release to that path. You might retain it for a low-risk class, run a shadow comparison, route the critical class back to baseline, or hand it to a human reviewer. These are scope-controlled choices; none claims production success.

Find the next experiment from the regression

Do not only say “train more.” Five new errors may come from label definitions, data distribution, a prompt template, context truncation, quantisation, adapter rank, retrieval interaction, or the evaluation harness. Inspect the error cluster first. Is it one input type, token budget, or tool path? If so, repair an inspectable cause and rerun exactly the same frozen cases. If not, keep the baseline and gather more bounded evidence rather than expanding the experiment to every user.

A release record that can be explained

Write the decision record as: “the overall candidate improves X; the critical slice regresses Y; therefore it does not release on path Z; the next reversible experiment is A; if B recurs, roll back or hand off.” That respects both statistical uncertainty and operational risk. It is stronger engineering judgement than “the score passed” because it names who bears an error, who approves, and when the system stops.

Evidence you could put in a portfolio

Build critical-slice-release-gate with:

  • fixtures/eval-cases.jsonl holding fictional general and critical cases, slice reasons, expected outcomes, and permitted handoffs.
  • docs/gate-definition.md recording the baseline, hard gate, sample limit, owner, and non-goal before a candidate runs.
  • evidence/slice-table.md with total score, per-slice counts, error examples, review notes, and the release or rejection decision.
  • scripts/compare-candidates.mjs freezing config and case version to produce a replayable comparison.
  • tests/release-gate.test.ts that fails when the overall score rises while a critical slice falls through its gate.

A valuable counterexample explicitly rejects a higher-scoring candidate and preserves why. It shows that you do not use an aggregate metric to hide a known risk.
